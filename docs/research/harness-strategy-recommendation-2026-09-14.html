Section 01The answer, first

You asked whether to shift to harness thinking. You have been doing harness engineering for at least a month; what is missing is not the concept, it is a bound on how far the concept is allowed to spread.

Three claims, in order of confidence.

1 · Do not discard CPP — the swap would be a downgrade

Superpowers is a methodology inside the agent session: brainstorm, worktree, plan, TDD, subagent review, finish. CPP is that plus the feedback half of a harness — CI, secret scanning, secrets management, deploy verification, cross-model review, an unbypassable necessity gate, wave orchestration with a durable mailbox, and three delegation lanes. In Böckeler's and Weng's sense of the word, Superpowers supplies feedforward controls and delegates every feedback control to you. Switching would trade roughly 80% of your harness for a methodology you already have an equivalent of.

2 · Your open backlog is harness engineering, already

Twelve of your sixteen open CPP issues are the same defect class: an instrument returning a confident verdict it is not entitled to. Not twelve unrelated bugs — one pattern, correctly identified, with #924 already filed as the machinery to end it. That issue is better specified than anything in the published literature on this subject. It is the thing you are asking whether to start.

3 · The real risk is the opposite of the one you are worried about

You are not behind on harness thinking. You are at risk of applying it to everything. Your Negative Control directive defines an instrument as "a test, a gate, a CI job, a pre-push hook, an acceptance check, a verification command in docs, a report, a probe, a monitor." Under that definition, all 2,365 of your test functions are instruments, and each needs a committed red case. That is unaffordable, and an unaffordable rule gets quietly dropped — which is worse than a narrower rule that holds. Section 04 proposes the bound.

On your own proposal — a symmetric counter-model, Codex against Claude in CPP and Claude against Codex in CxPP — it is the strongest idea in your message and it is better founded than I first credited. Both halves already exist and neither has ever run routinely: 0 of the last 200 merged CPP PRs carry the Codex review marker, and CxPP's Claude review is reachable only as an escalation after two failed attempts. You are not proposing a new capability; you are proposing to stop leaving two built ones switched off. That is Section 05.

#924's acceptance criteria deserve specific credit, because they contain a step that neither the published research nor the maintained frameworks require. The finding that should settle the strategic question

You are not evaluating whether to adopt a discipline the ecosystem has and you lack. You are further along on this specific discipline than the frameworks you are considering switching to. Superpowers ships pressure-tested skills; it does not ship a revert-and-reconfirm requirement. Spec Kit ships a constitution; it ships no negative control at all. The published 2026 consensus reached "verification > generation" as a slogan. #924 is the executable version.

The one caveat on that backlog

Your own issue-quality assessment is more sceptical than I am being, and it is worth honouring rather than skating past. It found CPP "has supplied misleading success signals and a cleanup regression," and judged that the portfolio is "overproducing locally convincing changes relative to demonstrated integrated outcomes." Its recurring-mismatch table names four families — execution-succeeded→work-succeeded, recorded→received, skipped→clean, correct-source→corrected-installation — which is the same pattern at a different altitude. Its conclusion was not "add governance"; it was "adopt the proportional issue contracts already delivered rather than building another governance layer." I agree with that, and Section 04 is written to comply with it.

Section 03Is Superpowers a harness?

You asked this directly, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a hedge: partly, and specifically the half you already have.

Superpowers has unmistakably absorbed harness thinking. Two pieces of evidence, both strong. The v6.3.0 release note (12 Aug 2026) reads: "Ceremony now scales to the task. Requests are classified as spike, bounded, or architectural; small tasks skip the two-document ritual. Every path still stops for your approval before implementation." And every delegation skill now carries a context-isolation principle, with Jesse Vincent's stated reason being harness-shaped rather than parallelism-shaped: "I don't want to pollute the orchestrator context with lots and lots of file reads that it doesn't need."

But map the two against Böckeler's split — feedforward controls anticipate what the agent needs; feedback controls detect and correct after the fact — and the gap is structural, not incidental.

But note what Thoughtworks attached to it in Vol.34: they rate Agent Skills Trial while explicitly cautioning "against unreviewed reuse of third-party skills, as they introduce serious supply chain security risks." Adopting a 283k-star markdown corpus wholesale converts a maintenance burden into a review burden. For a repo that already runs secret scanning and a tool-risk drift gate, that trade is not obviously positive.

What to take from Superpowers without taking Superpowers

The pressure-test technique. Vincent's first attempt at testing skills failed instructively: he quizzed subagents gameshow-style, they scored perfectly, and it told him nothing — a blind instrument for testing instruments. Realistic pressure (time pressure, sunk cost, simulated incidents) was what made the test able to fail. Your ELI5 gate has a no-bypass property asserted by tests/test_eli5_gate_not_bypassable.py. Has that property been tested against an agent under pressure to ship, rather than against a direct call? That is a cheap, high-value experiment and it does not require installing anything.

Section 04The cost nobody has priced

This is the part of the recommendation I would defend hardest, because it is the one that decides whether the discipline survives contact with the calendar.

Your Negative Control directive is correct and its scope is unbounded. Taken literally it applies to every one of 2,365 test functions, 61 scripts, 9 make verify sub-gates, every CI step, every probe in every wave helper, and every verification command in every docs file. Nobody has that much time. What happens to an unaffordable rule is not that it gets debated — it gets applied to whatever is in front of you and silently skipped everywhere else, which produces exactly the condition the rule exists to prevent: a population of instruments where some have controls and some do not, and no way to tell which from the outside.

#924 already senses this and scopes itself to "gates." But "gate" is not defined, and the seven consumers it names are the seven that happened to fail recently. That is a list that narrows, not a rule that generalizes — and by your own Tripwire vs Coverage Enumeration distinction, a list that narrows should be converted into a rule.

The bounding rule I would propose

An instrument needs a committed negative control when its verdict is consumed by a decision that will not independently re-derive the fact.

Three consequences, each testable:

  • A unit test whose failure would be caught by the next test in the same file does not need one. Its green is not load-bearing; the suite is.
  • A gate that lets work through — flow-finish-gate, delegated-run-check, the preflights, the drift checks, make verify itself — does need one. Nothing downstream re-derives what it asserted; that is the entire reason it exists.
  • A check whose green is read by a different session or a different repo always needs one, because the consumer cannot see the conditions that produced it. This is the case #924 makes about blind CPP gates passing bad work in kyle and CxPP.

Applied to your tree, that bound is affordable. It captures the nine sub-gates in make verify, the drift and currency checks, the wave-helper probes, the preflights, and the delegated-run checks — order tens, not thousands. It excludes the bulk of the 2,365 unit tests, which is the right answer: those are covered by whether the suite as a whole discriminates, which is a mutation-testing question, not a per-test one.

And it gives you a coverage denominator you can actually report, which today you cannot. Right now the honest statement is "3 of 133 test files contain explicit negative-control language." With the bound, the statement becomes "N of N load-bearing instruments have a registered, reverted-and-reconfirmed control" — a number that can be red.

A live example of why the bound matters

You already ship make negative-fixture-check, and it is genuinely good: it enforces that a fixture constructing an absence asserts the precondition, and it was born from a real defect (#695, where emptying PATH to remove git also removed ln, mkdir and bash, and the fail-open assertions still passed). But its detection is statically visible PATH replacement only — it says so in its own docstring. It reports ok - every constructed absence asserts its precondition. That sentence is broader than what the check can see. An absence constructed by chmod, by a stub that returns nonzero, by a container without a binary, or by a monkeypatched import is invisible to it and reports the same green. The negative-control gate is itself an instrument that overstates its own scope. It should be the first consumer of #924, ahead of #904.

Section 05The counter-model, symmetric and routine

Correction to the first draft of this memo

I wrote Section 05 as if the Codex review were part of the lifecycle. It is not. /flow:auto is nine steps and contains no cross-model stage at all; /flow:auto_codex is a separate, opt-in command that adds one. My claim that "Codex already sees the tests" was true only of runs that explicitly opted in — and the measurement below shows that is none of them. Your correction was right, and the evidence is stronger than you put it.

The measurement

/flow:auto_codex appends a ## Codex pre-PR review block to the PR body at Finish. That makes adoption countable.

0 / 200
merged CPP PRs carrying the Codex review marker
199 / 200
positive control — same query, a marker known to be present
5 / 102
merged CxPP PRs mentioning a Claude review (loose regex, likely over-counts)

The positive control matters: a zero from a broken query and a zero from a real absence look identical. Running the same extractor against a marker known to be in the bodies returns 199 of 200, so the bodies are populated and the regex works. The zero is real. The cross-model review stage has existed in CPP for months and has never once run on a merged PR.

The other half already exists too

CxPP ships claude-code-review — "Launch a bounded, read-only Claude Code session through the Claude Agent SDK to review the current issue-branch diff and advise Codex." The ownership boundary is drawn correctly: "Claude advises; Codex retains ownership of edits, tests, and lifecycle decisions."

But read its trigger clause:

"Use when the user requests a Claude or cross-model code review, or when flow-auto remains stuck after two materially distinct attempts and needs implementation support."

codex-power-pack · plugins/claude/skills/claude-code-review/SKILL.md

That is a rescue mechanism, not a standing adversary. It fires when the primary model is already failing — which is the one situation where it is least likely to be independent, because it arrives pre-loaded with the primary's framing of the blocker. And grep finds no reference to it anywhere in CxPP's flow-auto: it is not in the lifecycle either.

So your proposal is not "add a capability"

Both halves of the symmetric pair are already built. CPP has /codex:code_review wired into an opt-in lifecycle nobody invokes; CxPP has claude-code-review wired into nothing, reachable only as an escalation. What you are proposing is promotion: take two existing, unexercised capabilities and make them routine and reciprocal. That is a much smaller build than it sounds, and a much better-evidenced one — you are not betting that cross-model review works, you are ending a situation where you cannot possibly know, because it has never run.

Four design points, in order of how much they matter

1 · The decisive one

Default, not opt-in — but proportional

0 of 200 is the whole argument. An opt-in quality stage in a single-owner repo is a stage that does not exist; the moment there is a faster command next to it, the faster one wins every time. Fold the counter-model into the default lifecycle rather than keeping a parallel command.

Proportionally, though. Your tiering already says Tier 1 surgical work skips the spec pipeline; the same logic applies here — a counter-model pass on a one-line fix is the ceremony tax Superpowers removed in v6.3.0 and Spec Kit's critics complain about. Bind it to tier, not to a separate command name. The failure mode of auto_codex was not that it was heavy; it was that opting in was a decision, and decisions made per-invocation default to no.

2 · Your framing, and it is the right one

The counter-model is whichever model did not write the code

Codex counters Claude in CPP; Claude counters Codex in CxPP. This is correct and it is a stronger principle than "add a Codex review," because it survives the repos changing hands. The rule should be stated as a property, not as a tool name: the reviewing model must not be the implementing model. Then Qwen or Gemma can serve the role in a lane where one of them implements, and the rule still holds without an edit.

One thing to verify before relying on it: in CxPP, confirm Codex is genuinely the implementer and not an orchestrator delegating to a Claude session. Your 6 September comparison noted that CPP's codex:auto workers are "Claude sessions with a delegated implementation driver" — if any CxPP lane has the same shape, then "Claude reviews Codex" is Claude reviewing Claude's own output through a wrapper, and the independence the design depends on is not there.

3 · Where the diversity is load-bearing

Have the counter-model produce red cases, not only diff findings

This is the part of my earlier section that survives your correction, reframed. A reviewer handed a finished branch and a green suite reviews what is there; it is poorly placed to notice what is absent, because nothing marks a missing case. Reviewing the diff catches wrong assertions well and missing assertions badly.

So give the counter-model a second, cheaper product: for each instrument the change introduces or modifies, the input that should make it report the other verdict. That is verbatim what your Negative Control directive demands, sourced from a model that did not author the design — and it feeds #924 directly, since those cases are what gets registered as controls. The review stage and the control-generation stage are the same invocation with two outputs, not two stages.

4 · The trap in the current design

The primary model must not be the sole judge of the counter-model

auto_codex reports X accepted (fixed), Y rejected, Z deferred — and Claude decides the split. The adversary's findings pass through the author's judgement before anything happens. A counter-model whose every finding can be rejected by the model it is checking is a counter-model that can be silently no-opped, and the run still reports as having had cross-model review.

You do not need to remove the judgement — the primary usually is right about a false positive. You need the ratio to be visible and to be a thing you can look at across runs. If Y is consistently near X+Y, the stage is decorative. That is a measurement you currently cannot take, because the denominator is zero.

The counter-model stage is itself an instrument

By your own directive it does not get to ship without a red case. The question to answer before promoting it: what input makes this stage report "findings"? A stage that has only ever returned clean is indistinguishable from a stage that cannot return anything else — and with 0 of 200, you have no observation either way. The cheapest control is a seeded branch with a known defect of a class the reviewer should catch; assert the stage reports it, then assert it reports clean on the same branch with the defect removed. Both directions, because a reviewer wedged at "findings" is equally useless.

And it stays self-measuring

Once it runs by default, count on each run: how many of the counter-model's proposed red cases were already covered by the implementer's tests. Near-total overlap means the two models are not failing independently on your workload — and that is not a disappointing result, it is the single most valuable thing this design can tell you, because the whole premise rests on an independence assumption that nobody has tested. Both models have read your conventions and share training corpora; correlation is the expected failure, not an exotic one.

Section 06What changes in Codex Power Pack

Less than you might expect, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of priority.

CxPP is downstream. Your 6 September comparison found the inspected CxPP HEAD at 2026-08-07 with make codex-skills-currency-check failing on 59 changed or missing files across 20 skills — and noted that the drift was broader than the two missing wave commands: start, auto, finish, merge, check, doctor and their helpers had all moved. CxPP's 28-day flow balance is +19 with 21 open, most of that program scaffolding rather than defects.

So the recommendation is a constraint on where the negative-control machinery lands, not a separate CxPP workstream:

  • Build the #924 helper in scripts/, so it symlinks into ~/.claude/scripts with no install step — as #924 already specifies — and so it flows to CxPP through the existing generation bridge rather than being reimplemented there. A negative-control framework that exists in two hand-maintained copies is a framework with a drift problem, which is the defect class it was built to end.
  • Register controls for the shared helpers first. The gates that fail in CPP are the same gates CxPP runs, because CxPP's skills are generated from CPP's commands. #924 makes this point about kyle; it applies equally here and the fix is free if the helper lives in the right directory.
  • Refresh the currency drift before adding anything. Adding a new gate family to a surface that is 59 files behind means the generated CxPP copies will be the second thing that is stale. Close the currency gap, then generate.
  • The Codex case-list stage is CPP-side only. It calls Codex as a reviewer; it does not need a CxPP skill. Do not build a mirror of it until the yield measurement says it is worth having.

Section 07What I would not do

  • Do not migrate to Superpowers, Spec Kit-as-primary, or any other framework as a replacement. You would lose CI, secrets, security scanning, deploy verification, wave orchestration, delegation lanes and the necessity gate, to gain a methodology you have. If you want a component, take the component.
  • Do not add a governance layer. Your own assessment's closing recommendation was "adopt the proportional issue contracts already delivered rather than building another governance layer." The bounding rule in Section 04 is deliberately a narrowing of an existing directive, not a new one. If the recommendation in this memo grows a new tier system, I have got it wrong.
  • Do not treat the 12 blind gates as a burn-down. #924 is explicit that the framework must not be bundled with the fixes it validates, "because the framework's evidence would be produced by the thing it is meant to check." That constraint is correct and it means the gates get fixed one at a time, after the machinery lands, not in a sprint.
  • Do not prune command families on instinct. The skillmaxxing evidence is real — 18 families plus a generated Codex mirror carries a metadata tax in every session — but Microsoft's own procedure is measure first: run the scenarios without the artifact, score them, let the measured failures define the scope. Pruning without that measurement is guessing with extra confidence. If you want the surface smaller, measure it; do not eyeball it.
  • Do not start this in Kyle. Kyle is at +114 flow balance with 112 open. A new discipline introduced into the repo with the most churn will be evaluated against the noisiest possible background. CPP is the right pilot precisely because it is quiet.

Section 08Sequence

Ordered so that each step's evidence does not depend on the step after it.

First

Land #924's machinery against negative-fixture-check, not #904

#924 nominates #904 as the cleanest first consumer. I would use check-negative-fixture-preconditions.py instead, for one reason: it is the instrument whose blindness is most consequential, because it is the one currently certifying that your other instruments are honest, and its ok message claims a scope wider than its detection. Registering a control that fails against a chmod-constructed or stub-constructed absence — then widening the check, then reverting and reconfirming — proves the framework on the instrument whose failure would hide the most. #904 becomes the second consumer.

Second

Write the bounding rule into the directive, with its exclusions

Add the "consumed by a decision that will not independently re-derive the fact" test to the Negative Control section, together with the explicit carve-out for unit tests whose green is not individually load-bearing. Without the carve-out the rule is unaffordable; with it you get a denominator. This is a narrowing edit, not a new layer.

Third

Give the counter-model stage a red case, then promote it to the default lifecycle

In that order. Seed a branch with a known defect, prove the stage reports it, prove it reports clean without it — then fold it into /flow:auto bound to tier, and retire /flow:auto_codex as a separate command. Keeping the opt-in variant alongside a default is how you get back to 0 of 200.

Record per run: findings raised, accepted, rejected, and how many proposed red cases the implementer's tests already covered. Ten issues is enough to tell "the models fail independently" from "the counter-model is agreeing with itself."

Fourth

Mirror it in CxPP with the roles reversed

claude-code-review already exists and already draws the ownership boundary correctly. The change is its trigger: promote it from escalation-on-stuck to a routine stage in flow-auto, bound to tier, with Claude as the counter-model because Codex is the implementer there. Verify first that no CxPP lane is a Claude session wearing a Codex label — if one is, that lane has no counter-model and the symmetry is nominal.

Then, and only then

Refresh CxPP currency and generate the controls downstream

Close the 59-file drift, regenerate, and let CxPP inherit the registered controls through the bridge. If the Codex stage earned its place in step three, mirror it; if it did not, do not.

Deferred

Measure the command surface before deciding whether to shrink it

The skillmaxxing question is real but it is not urgent, and answering it badly is worse than answering it late. It also becomes much easier once the negative-control framework exists, because "does removing this change an outcome" is the same shape of question.

Section 09Where I could be wrong

Stated so you can weigh the recommendation rather than accept it.

  • The single-owner maintenance argument is the strongest case against me, and I have not fully answered it. 95 command files, 61 scripts, 133 test files, 2,365 tests, a generated Codex mirror and three delegation lanes is a large surface for one person, and every surface is itself subject to the blindness this memo is about. A reasonable person could read "12 of 16 open issues are blind instruments" as the harness is too big to keep honest rather than the review culture is working. I read it the second way because all twelve were found, and found fast — but that is a judgement, not a measurement, and I did not find a way to test it.
  • I did not run your test suite. Every claim about test quality here is structural — file counts, grep for negative-control language, reading the fixture gate's own docstring. "3 of 133 files contain explicit negative-control language" is a fact about vocabulary, not about whether those tests discriminate. A mutation run would tell you far more than this memo does about the 2,365, and I would trust it over my inference.
  • The bounding rule in Section 04 is untested. I believe it is affordable and I have not enumerated the instruments it captures. If the real count comes out in the hundreds rather than the tens, the rule needs another narrowing and my "affordable" claim was wrong.
  • The Codex case-list stage may be redundant with what Step 5 already catches. That is precisely why Section 05 is built as a measurement rather than a proposal. If the yield is near zero I would expect you to delete it, and I would not regard that as a failure of the idea.
  • I have not inspected Superpowers' source. The capability table is built from its README, release notes, the maintainer's own interviews, and Thoughtworks' assessment — not from reading the skills. If it has grown CI or deploy integration that those sources do not mention, the comparison understates it.
  • Kyle is out of scope here and it is where your actual problem is. +114 flow balance, 112 open, 259 openings against 183 mainline commits in a week. Nothing in this memo addresses that, and if you only have appetite for one initiative, the evidence says Kyle's integration and acceptance practices deserve it more than CPP's instrument controls do.
